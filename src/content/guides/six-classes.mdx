The Chinese system — 六大茶类 — classifies **by process, not by colour**: 绿茶 green, 黄茶 yellow, 白茶 white, 青茶/乌龙茶 oolong, 红茶 black, 黑茶 dark. It is the modern classification this atlas uses. Attribution details vary; the atlas does not hang the system on one name.

## The false friend

English *black tea* is Chinese **红茶** *hóngchá*, “red tea” — named for the liquor.

Chinese **黑茶** *hēichá*, “black tea”, is the post-fermented class the atlas files as `dark`.

So *black tea* and *black tea* are two different teas depending on which language you are standing in. The glossary entry [Red tea / black tea](../glossary#hong-cha-hei-cha) is the one-line version. Every black and dark entry links it.

Puer’s relationship to 黑茶 is contested. Chinese standards give puer its own designation rather than treating it as a straightforward subset. This atlas files sheng and shou with the dark teas **by process**, and says so on those pages. It does not cite a standard number.

## What is not a class

**Scented** is not one of the six. 花茶 is a treatment applied to a base tea. Jasmine pearls in this atlas are a green tea that was scented; the record carries `baseCategory: green`.

**Hojicha** is a green tea that is brown. Oxidation 0, transformation kind `roast`. The cup colour is fire.

**An Ji Bai Cha** is a green tea. “White” is the cultivar’s pale leaf.

Oolong is not “semi-oxidised” as a fixed value. The class spans roughly 15 to 70 in this atlas alone. Wenshan Baozhong and Dong Fang Mei Ren are barely the same category.

White tea is not unprocessed. Withering *is* processing, and it is the longest and most weather-dependent step in the atlas.

The storage guide’s line still holds across classes: *Most teas are food. A few teas are projects.* Greens, jade oolong, and matcha are food. Aged sheng, some whites, and a handful of dark teas are projects.

Herbal “teas” are not Camellia sinensis. Out of scope.
